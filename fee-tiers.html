"Stablecoins are cheap" is a fee-tier story

The rail costs almost nothing. The doors cost the rest — and a fee schedule most senders never read decides which route actually wins.
The claim everyone repeats

Stablecoins are supposed to make cross-border payments cheap. The argument, in its usual form: they settle in seconds, cost fractions of a cent, and skip the correspondent-banking chain that makes a wire slow and expensive. On the narrow question of moving a stablecoin from one wallet to another, that's true. Measured live on the Singapore-to-Philippines route, sending S$5,000: the fee for moving the coin itself is 0.10% of the transfer.

That number is the entire public narrative. It is not the number that decides whether a real transfer is cheap.

The rail costs a fraction of a percent. The doors cost the rest. Every point of that is a published fee or a real, live market gap — not an estimate.

The correction

The obvious objection: buying it now pays the worst price on both legs. What if you wait for your own price instead?

An earlier version of this measurement said that changes everything: waiting for your price beat the best fiat provider by roughly 3×. That was wrong, and it's worth saying exactly how, because the mistake is more instructive than the corrected number.

The earlier draft modelled waiting for your price as free execution — no fee, on the theory that a posted order doesn't cross the spread. It doesn't cross the spread. It still pays a fee. Independent Reserve publishes no discount at all for waiting — a posted order there pays the same flat 0.50% a market order does. Coins.ph's own schedule charges 0.10% to wait, against 0.15% to buy now. Once both legs are priced at their real, published fee schedule instead of zero, the gap barely moves: the same S$5,000 transfer costs about 0.80% waiting for your price — a saving of about 5 hundredths of a percentage point, not the 3× the earlier draft claimed.

At published fees, the stablecoin route loses to the best fiat provider in both execution styles, across every size this site measures. This correction stays on the record, not because it's flattering, but because a site whose whole claim is "checked, not assumed" has to survive checking its own headline result. The mistake was an assumption; the fix was reading the actual fee schedules.
The one route where it's close

Of the four routes this site prices at the same rigor, three lose to the best fiat provider in almost every measured hour — Singapore, Australia and New Zealand to the Philippines all sit at or near a 0% win rate buying it now. The United States to Mexico is different: waiting for your price there wins 43.7% of measured hours at small sizes.

Why that route specifically: Bitso, the exchange handling the Mexican side, runs materially tighter fees than Coins.ph's schedule, and publishes its volume-tier schedule as real, machine-readable data — nothing scraped off a page. That's a structural difference in what the two exchanges charge, not a fluke of one hour's prices.

A fee-tier question, not a verdict

None of this says stablecoins are a bad way to move money. It says the "stablecoins are cheap" argument, made about the rail alone, answers a question nobody sending real money is actually asking. The real question — does it beat what I'd pay otherwise — depends on a fee schedule most senders never see: the volume discount every exchange publishes and almost nobody reads.

That crossover isn't a guess here. It's measured: for Singapore to the Philippines, the stablecoin route only starts winning once a sender is running real payments-company volume, not retail — S$3.6M a month and up buying it now, S$2.7M a month if waiting for your price. That's the volume that unlocks Independent Reserve's discounted 0.14%/0.18% tiers, down from the 0.50% base rate everyone starts on. Below it, stablecoins lose, every time.
