# "Stablecoins are cheap" is a fee-tier story

*Draft for D2, margin.wiki v1 freeze and ship brief. Every number below is
cited to the file it comes from. Rewrite the voice before this ships —
the job of this draft is to get every number and citation right, not to
sound like anything final.*

## 1. The claim everyone repeats

Stablecoins are supposed to make moving money across borders cheap. The
argument, in its usual form: crypto rails settle in seconds, cost fractions
of a cent, and cut out the correspondent-banking chain that makes a wire
transfer slow and expensive. On the narrow question of what it costs to
move a stablecoin from one wallet to another, that's true. Measured live on
the Singapore→Philippines route, sending S$5,000: the network fee for
moving one USDT is **2.6 bps** — 0.026% of the transfer.
[[README.md:16](README.md)]

That number is the entire public narrative. It is also not the number that
determines whether a real transfer is cheap.

## 2. Where the cost actually sits: the two doors

A transfer that starts in a bank account and ends in one has to cross two
doors — buying the stablecoin (on-ramp) and selling it back (off-ramp) —
and that is where essentially all of the real cost sits. The full
decomposition, same route, same S$5,000, instant execution:

```
on-ramp basis (USDT vs USD mid, SG)   −12.0 bps   ← a gain
on-ramp fee (buying it now)           +50.0
network fee (moving it, 1 USDT)        +2.6       ← the entire public narrative
off-ramp basis (USDT vs USD mid, PH)  +29.2
off-ramp fee (buying it now)          +15.0
                                      ───────
                                       84.8 bps   vs the best ordinary way at 65.9 bps
```
[[README.md:11-21](README.md)]

The rail costs 2.6 bps. The doors cost 82. Every point of that 82 is a
published, verified fee or a real, live market gap — not an estimate.
[[METHODOLOGY.md, "The decomposition"](METHODOLOGY.md)]

## 3. The maker myth

The obvious objection: instant execution ("buying it now") pays the worst
price on both legs. What if you post an order and wait instead?

An earlier version of this measurement said that changes everything: maker
execution beat the best ordinary option roughly 3×. That result was wrong,
and it's worth saying exactly how, because the mistake is more instructive
than the corrected number.

The earlier draft modelled maker execution as *free* — zero fee, on the
theory that a posted order doesn't cross the spread. It doesn't cross the
spread. It still pays a fee. Independent Reserve publishes **no maker
discount at all** — a posted order there pays the same flat 0.50% a market
order does. Coins.ph's Pro VIP0 maker fee is 0.10%, against 0.15% for
taking. Once both legs are priced at their real, published maker schedule
instead of zero, the gap closes almost entirely: the same S$5,000 transfer
costs **~79.6 bps** at maker execution — a savings of about 5 bps over
taking, not the 3× the earlier draft claimed.
[[README.md:26-35](README.md)] [[METHODOLOGY.md, "The two execution regimes"](METHODOLOGY.md)]

**At published base-tier fees, the stablecoin route loses to the best
ordinary way to send money in *both* execution regimes, across every size
this site measures.** Taker: ~78–139 bps. Maker: ~73–134 bps. Against a
best ordinary option running ~59–89 bps over the same window.
[[METHODOLOGY.md, "The two execution regimes"](METHODOLOGY.md)]

This correction stays on the record, not because it's flattering, but
because a site whose whole claim is "checked, not assumed" has to survive
checking its own headline result. The mistake was an assumption (maker is
free); the fix was reading the actual fee schedules.
[[METHODOLOGY.md:103](METHODOLOGY.md)]

## 4. The one route where it's close

Of the four routes this site prices at the same rigor, three lose to the
best ordinary way in the overwhelming majority of measured hours —
Singapore→Philippines, Australia→Philippines, and New Zealand→Philippines
all sit at or near a 0% win rate for the stablecoin route. The United
States→Mexico route is different: at maker execution, it wins roughly 44%
of measured hours at small sizes.
[[data/corridor_summary.json, generated by tools/emit_corridor_summary.py](data/corridor_summary.json)]

Why that route specifically: Bitso, the Mexican exchange handling the
off-ramp leg, runs materially tighter fees than Coins.ph's schedule, and
its published fee-tier structure is public and machine-readable — no
scraping required, the volume-tier schedule comes straight back from its
own API. [[tools/check_fees.py, parse_bitso_tiers](tools/check_fees.py)]
That's a real, structural difference in what the two off-ramp venues
charge, not a fluke of one hour's order book.

## 5. The conclusion: a fee-tier question, not a verdict

None of this says stablecoins are a bad way to move money. It says the
"stablecoins are cheap" argument, made about the rail alone, is answering a
question nobody sending real money is actually asking. The real question —
does it beat what I'd pay otherwise — depends on a fee schedule most
people sending money never see: the volume-tier discount every exchange
publishes and almost nobody reads.

That crossover — the trading volume at which a route stops losing and
starts winning — isn't a guess here. It's measured: for
Singapore→Philippines, it's roughly SGD 3.6M of monthly volume at taker
execution, or SGD 2.7M at maker, the volume that unlocks Independent
Reserve's discounted 0.14%/0.18% tiers.
[[data/volume_crossover.json, generated by tools/emit_volume_crossover.py](data/volume_crossover.json)]
That's a real payments company's volume, not a retail sender's — which is
exactly the shape of finding this dataset exists to produce: not
"stablecoins good" or "stablecoins bad," but a real number for the volume
at which the answer changes.
